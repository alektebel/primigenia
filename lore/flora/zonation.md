## Zonation, coast to summit

Salt-secreting Salvisca covers the spray zone, while Cinta de marea occupies the tidal shelf and sheltered sea-arch pools, exposed and submerged by shelf tides reaching about 3.2 m at springs (F13, F20, F21). Inland, the wet western slopes carry 12–22 m Nieblón fog-drip forest with vines, ferns, epiphytic mats and decomposers; the eastern start valley supports drought-dormant Paja dulce meadow despite frequent dawn fog (F8, F18). Trenza de ribera follows freshwater channels, and the single 28–32 m Madre de agua stands at the spring impounded by the rock-spire dyke (F21). Lava surfaces support crusts, pioneer grasses and insect-catching rosettes according to moisture and soil development (F7, F9). Geothermal patches interrupt these belts with sulfur-rich microbial crust communities (F23). Ferns and moss occupy illuminated cave entrances and waterfall spray; fungi extend into dark tubes where imported organic matter sustains them (F9). High slopes become wind-pruned vegetation and giant rosettes, with winter snow above about 1,750 m and surface UV reaching 1.4× Earth's at 2,000 m, below the 2,050 m summit (F7, F14, F15, F19). Independently of elevation, Hierroflor marks metal-rich patches on the crater floor and along its bent ejecta ray; the crater's altitude is unspecified (F22).

## Primary succession on young lava

After lava cools, Ceniza roja establishes on suitable damp rock, using photosynthesis, mineral-weathering acids and the shared nitrogen-fixing symbiont to accumulate dust and organic matter (F7, F9, F24). Grama de ceniza roots in water-holding cracks; Lengua de lava supplements mineral nutrition with insects at nutrient-poor seeps. Roots exploit existing fractures rather than splitting intact basalt. Litter and decomposition deepen soil, enabling shrubs and, on sufficiently wet sheltered western sites, Nieblón forest (F18, F19). Dry eastern sites may remain grassland; exposed ridges, alpine ground and chemically hostile geothermal patches follow different trajectories. The spring giant depends on the dyke aquifer, rather than representing a universal final stage (F21). Dark lava tubes receive organic matter from outside and support decomposers, not photosynthetic succession (F9). Recovery has no fixed timetable: substrate age, water, wind, erosion and chemistry maintain a mosaic, with locally metal-rich crater patches retaining specialist vegetation after 12,000 years (F22, F23). All forms share the seeded biochemistry and derive from island founders and their radiation (F24, F27).
